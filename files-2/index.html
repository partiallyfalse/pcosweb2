What is PCOS?

PCOS stands for Polycystic Ovary Syndrome. It's one of the most common hormonal conditions in people who have ovaries — affecting roughly 1 in 10 of them during their reproductive years. It's not rare, it's not your fault, and it's very manageable once you understand what's going on.

At its core, PCOS is a condition where hormones that normally work together in careful balance — estrogen, progesterone, and androgens — get a little out of sync. Most people with PCOS produce slightly higher androgen levels than typical, and many also have some degree of insulin resistance, meaning the body needs more insulin than usual to keep blood sugar steady.

Despite the name, PCOS doesn't actually require cysts on the ovaries. The name comes from how ovaries can look on an ultrasound — sometimes showing many small fluid-filled follicles — but plenty of people with PCOS never have this, and some people with those follicles don't have PCOS at all.

How it's usually diagnosed

Doctors typically diagnose PCOS when at least two of these three are present:

  • Irregular or absent ovulation (which shows up as irregular periods)
  • Signs of higher androgen levels (in bloodwork or physical symptoms)
  • Polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound

No one knows the exact single cause. It tends to run in families and seems to come from a mix of genetics, insulin response, and hormone regulation — not from anything anyone did or didn't do.
